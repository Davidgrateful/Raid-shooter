The diagnosis

Three things were making it read as a web page

The brief asked for a game that says “I want to play” in three seconds. The old build was not far off — the art, the palette and the ambition were already there. What worked against it was structural, and it came down to three specific faults rather than a general lack of polish.

The menu was a list of buttons

Five grey rectangles under the logo. No player, no ship, no progression — nothing on screen belonged to the person looking at it, and every option carried the same weight as every other.

The chrome was scattered to the edges

Feedback, invite, streak, weekly gift, sponsors, comms and the cup all lived as separate floating pills pinned to different corners. That arrangement is what a web page looks like.

Texture was competing with content

Scanlines ran edge to edge at every second pixel, over the ship, the HUD and every panel. It read as grime on the screen rather than as atmosphere behind it.

Main menu

The whole change, in one drag

The canvas engine still paints the starfield and the drifting hangar traffic; it just stops drawing its own menu chrome and hands the screen to an HTML command centre that sits inside the game world rather than on top of it.

Reading order
Fixed and deliberate: call sign and tier, then the ship, then level and XP, then DEPLOY — the only element on the screen that glows.
The ship
Not an image. It runs the pilot’s real draw function from the engine’s character definitions, in their equipped hull colour, with their trail tinting the engine wash and their drone in orbit. What sits there is what launches.
Navigation
A command terminal with cyan edge lighting on the active slot, rather than a website sidebar. Five real destinations, no filler.
The ops column
Every card answers one of “what can I do / what can I earn / where do I rank”. The floating pills folded into it; nothing was added to fill space.
Hull telemetry
The pilot’s real definition numbers as three comparable meters, so swapping hulls in the hangar has a visible consequence on the main screen.

Phone, portrait

The biggest single fix is the one with the least design in it

Raids are played in landscape, so the old build showed a rotate prompt to any touch device held in portrait — on every screen, including the menu and the leaderboard. A player opening the game one-handed hit a wall before seeing anything.

Only gameplay needs landscape. The rotate prompt now appears when a raid actually starts, and portrait gets a real layout: top status, ship, identity, progression, DEPLOY, ops cards, and a five-slot bottom bar within thumb reach.

In-run HUD

One row across the top became three corners

The old HUD put health, progress, score, best and combo in a single left-to-right strip, and listed every powerup in the bottom-left corner whether or not you had one. Both screenshots below are pinned to the same mid-run state — same score, same chain, same hull, same active effects — so the comparison is about the design, not about who got luckier with spawns.

Top left
Survival. Hull with its integrity number and a hue that walks red as it drops, dash charge, and field-kit chips labelled by the key that fires them.
Top centre
The number you are playing for, large. The chain multiplier sits directly beneath it, heating from gold to white as it climbs, over a decay bar that is the run’s actual tension.
Top right
Progress and active effects. Powerups appear as chips that drain along their own bottom edge — and only when they are running. An always-on list of things you do not have was pushing the real ones off the read.
Event feed
Three lines, top right, about two seconds each: a level cleared, an elite down, a chain milestone. Trash kills never reach it — a line per mob would be wallpaper.
The middle
Left empty. Everything is anchored to a corner and clear of the safe-area insets, so the part of the screen where the shooting happens stays clear.

Shooterboard

You should be able to tell who is winning from across the room

The old podium gave the champion, the runner-up and third the same card at the same size, which makes the top of a competitive ladder read as a three-way tie. Below them, every row looked identical whether it was rank four or rank four hundred.

The champion
Taller, wider, lit, with a bigger avatar and a bigger number — and their own light spilling out of the card.
Three bands
Top three on the podium, the top ten carrying a lit cyan edge, and everyone else plain. Readable without counting.
Your row
Gold edge, gold fill, a YOU marker — overriding both bands, so you can find yourself in a thousand rows at a glance.
On a phone
The champion keeps full width; second and third pair up beneath. The old stack cost three screens of scrolling before a single ranked row appeared.

Armory

A place you outfit a ship, not a place you make a transaction

Same catalogue, same prices, same purchase flow. What changed is that items now carry a rarity the eye can scan before it reads a price, the selected tab lights up like every other active thing in the interface, and settlement is a footnote rather than the headline.

Rarity
Derived from the catalogue’s own price spread and recomputed whenever it loads — so it stays honest if you reprice anything, and shows nothing at all when there is no spread to read.
Right column
Rarity above price. A player scanning a storefront sees what kind of thing it is before what it costs, which is the order they actually shop in.
Active tab
Lit, rather than marked with a > that also shifted the label off centre.

End of run

Lead with what the run changed, not what it scored

The end of a run is the biggest progression moment the game has, and the old card reported a number at the player. The new one pays them first: the XP the run banked, the pilot level bar it moved, and how far the next level is — then the stats, then one obvious way back in.

The outcome is also stated in colour before a single figure: gold for a record run, red for a hull lost.

The system underneath

Five hues, three faces, one corner

None of the screens above were designed one at a time. They are all drawn from a single token layer in globals.css — 28 tokens, shared by the React overlays and, through matching canvas helpers, by the engine’s own buttons and cards. This document is built from the same tokens.

The rule that keeps it coherent: every colour carries meaning. Nothing here is tinted for decoration, which is why the interface can use six hues without looking like a parade.

Geometry

One motif ties the whole interface together: a corner bitten out of the top-left and bottom-right of every surface. Panels, cards, canvas buttons, the DEPLOY face, the reward drops and the boxes on this page all share it, so a hangar button and a menu card visibly belong to the same machine. All three typefaces are self-hosted through next/font, which keeps the site’s existing content-security policy untouched.

Verification

What was actually checked

✓
Every screen, every breakpoint Ten engine screens driven through Playwright at five viewports — desktop, laptop, tablet, phone portrait and phone landscape. All navigate; no console errors; no horizontal scroll anywhere.
✓
Lint parity 141 problems before the redesign, 141 after — byte-identical. The change introduced none of its own.
✓
Types and engine syntax tsc --noEmit clean · node --check across all 28 engine files
✓
The reward path, end to end A cup cosmetic granted mid-session is announced exactly once, marked seen, and never shown again — verified by driving the real flow, not by reading the code.

One real bug turned up while reviewing the diff, and it is worth naming because it is the kind this sort of change causes. Gating the canvas menu behind a flag also trapped the tournament-reward congratulation inside that gate — so a cosmetic a player had won on the board would have been granted silently and never announced. It is now resolved on both paths and surfaced in the ops column as a reward drop.

Two calls worth a second opinion

Judgement calls, not defaults

Both of these are reversible in minutes. They are flagged because they are product decisions wearing design clothes, and they are yours to make.

Naming

MARKET is now ARMORY

The brief asked for a marketplace that feels like an equipment armory rather than a trading interface, so the nav slot, the screen title and its subtitle all changed. Purchase behaviour is untouched.

The risk is familiarity: returning players know the word MARKET. If you would rather keep it, it is a three-string revert.

Economy

Rarity is inferred, not authored

The catalogue carries no rarity field, so rarity is derived from the spread of prices actually in it. The first attempt used fixed price bands and every pilot came out COMMON, which is why it works this way.

If rarity should be a real property of an item rather than a read of its price, it wants a field in the catalogue — a small change, and the display is already built for it.
